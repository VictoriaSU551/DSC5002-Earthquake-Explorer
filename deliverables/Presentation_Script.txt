Opening | introduce the research
Hello, and thank you for stopping by. We are the DSC5002 project team at City University of Hong Kong. Our project is called Inside the Earth. When we see a cluster of earthquakes on a world map, what can that map tell us, and what is still hidden? We combined time, location, magnitude and depth to make those patterns easier to explore. Our goal is to describe the recorded catalogue, not to predict the next earthquake.

Methods | point to section 1
First, we used a fixed USGS snapshot containing 46,463 unique earthquake records, from 2000 through 2025, with preferred magnitude at least five. All final analyses use the same records. We checked a teammate's larger extract and identified 60 non-earthquake events, so those were not part of our earthquake sample. We calculated spherical distances to PB2002 boundary arcs and used clearly specified regional boxes. For comparisons across time, we allowed for the one-day difference between the two thirteen-year periods.

Spatial results | point to sections 2 and 3
The map on the left shows the familiar concentration around the Pacific and other mapped boundaries. The distance curve makes this more precise: 70.15 percent of the records lie within one hundred kilometres of a PB2002 boundary, and the median distance is 59.68 kilometres. This is a description of proximity to our selected map. It is not a test against a spatial null model, and PB2002 does not represent every active fault.

Time and regions | point to sections 4 and 5
Next, the middle column shows how recorded counts vary through time. The annual maximum is 2,701 in 2011. Comparing 2013 to 2025 with 2000 to 2012, the exposure-adjusted rate is 6.63 percent lower at magnitude five or above, and 15.42 percent lower at magnitude six or above. Regional changes differ: Indonesia and the Philippines decrease by 25.58 percent, while the Mediterranean and Himalaya group increases by 8.82 percent. These are changes in catalogue counts. They should not be read as changes in hazard, especially because the regions differ in area.

Depth and magnitude | point to sections 6, 7 and 8
Depth adds another dimension. Globally, 83.20 percent of the records are shallow. Yet the deep-event share is 11.76 percent in Tonga, Kermadec and Fiji, compared with 5.01 percent in Japan and Kuril. The two local profiles use the same distance and depth scales, with zero at the mapped trench. The Tonga profile contains 186 deep events out of 640, while the northern Tohoku profile contains 21 out of 351. These are selected local swaths, not representative samples of entire regions. The magnitude charts show that larger events are much less frequent: 387 records reach magnitude seven, and only 28 reach magnitude eight. We did not assume that the threshold proves catalogue completeness.

Sequences and animation | point to sections 9 and the linked frame
Finally, we compared three major-event windows using the same five-hundred-kilometre radius and thirty days on each side, excluding the anchor itself. The after-window counts are 143 for Sumatra, 302 for Maule and 563 for Tohoku. Around sixty to seventy-one percent of those post-window records fall in the first three days. Conclusion | point to the bottom strip
To conclude, the project makes boundary proximity, regional depth contrasts and event sequences visible within one consistent catalogue. The main caution is comparability. The composition of preferred magnitude types and the availability of reporting fields change over time, so a count difference alone cannot identify a physical change in earthquake activity. Our contribution is a reproducible, interactive description with those limits made explicit. Thank you for your attention. We would be happy to discuss the figures or demonstrate the animation.
